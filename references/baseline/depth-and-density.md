# Depth and Density

How much to put on a slide, and — more importantly — **where**.

## Depth is a budget, not a constant

"One slide, one job" is about focus, not word count. A deck is not uniformly sparse. Almost every deck has
three to five **load-bearing slides** — the ones carrying the argument the deck exists to make — and a
majority of **supporting slides** that only need to land one fact.

- **Load-bearing slides: go deep.** These earn real density: full sentences, sub-points, concrete examples,
  named specifics, actual constraints. If the reader remembers only these, the deck worked. Under-filling
  them is the more common failure and by far the more expensive one — a thin slide on the one thing that
  mattered wastes the whole deck.
- **Supporting slides: one idea, few words.** A number, a list of names, a timeline, a code sample. No
  sub-points, no explanatory paragraph, no note at the bottom.
- **Decide which is which before writing any slide**, straight from the audience shift. Then spend the budget.

Rough word count per 16:9 slide:

| Slide role | Words | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Supporting | under ~40 | one dominant object, nothing else |
| Normal | 40–80 | a claim plus three or four parallel items |
| Load-bearing | 80–150 | claim, framing sentence, items with sub-lines |

Above ~150 words you are past the point where anyone reads it on screen — split.

## Split, don't compress

When a slide is too full, add a second slide. Do not shrink the type, and do not trim the clauses that make
the point land. Two focused slides always beat one crammed one, and slides are cheap.

This cuts both ways. Compressing a load-bearing slide destroys the reason it existed. If a supporting slide's
body only restates its title, delete the body — or the slide.

## Ask how the deck will be consumed

This shifts the depth calculus more than anything else, and it is usually unstated:

- **Presented live** — the slide is a prompt. Detail belongs in what the speaker says.
- **Sent as a file** with no presenter (PDF, link, forwarded to someone who was not in the room) — the slide
  has to carry the detail, because there is no narration to carry it. Everything the reader needs in order to
  act must be on the page: what the thing is, what is being asked, by when.
- **Both** — the common case. Write so it reads alone, structure so it scans in a room.

Ask if it is not stated. A deck that will be forwarded needs a self-sufficient opening (what is this, who is
it for) and a self-sufficient ask (what do I do, by when).

## Signals a slide needs MORE depth

- A senior reader would immediately ask "why?" and the slide has no answer.
- It asserts something contestable and shows no evidence.
- It names a decision without naming the trade-off.
- It is the slide the whole deck exists for, and it fits in a tweet.
- The audience is being asked to do real work off the back of it.

## Signals a slide needs LESS

- Two ideas competing to be the dominant read.
- A paragraph the presenter will read aloud verbatim.
- Sub-points hanging off a slide whose job is a single number.
- Content that is there because it was researched, not because it argues.
- A closing note that restates the title in different words.

## Ground depth in real artefacts

Depth is only worth anything if it is true and specific. Before writing a technical or internal deck, read
the actual source: the repository, the tickets, the READMEs, the config. The details that make a deck
credible — the duplicated file names, the real API snippet, the install promise a proposal would break — come
from there. Invented or generic illustrations are obvious to exactly the audience you are trying to convince.
